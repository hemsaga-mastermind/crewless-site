Why we won't promise you a trading algorithm

2026-09-25 Pre-proof build-log Sourced

Most of the industry sells the same thing: a proven algorithm that quietly, consistently beats the market. We won't — and not out of modesty. No one can honestly promise a consistently-profitable trading algorithm — the research on why is unambiguous. What we can promise is the discipline the fantasy skips. So here's the hard truth, with our sources, and what we do instead.

Four reasons no one can promise it

01

Edges decay by nature. Andrew Lo's Adaptive Markets Hypothesis describes profitable strategies going through an evolutionary cycle — discovered, exploited, crowded, extinct — as the population of competitors changes (Lo, SSRN). An edge isn't a permanent machine; it's a temporary advantage the market evolves against. We've watched it happen to our own signals within days of finding them.

02

Backtests lie by default. This is the core of Bailey & López de Prado's work: the more strategy variants you test, the more likely the best one is pure luck. As few as a handful of trials start producing false positives, and a backtest Sharpe of 2.0 across a hundred variants can deflate toward 0.5 once you correct for the search (Bailey & López de Prado). A beautiful backtest is weak evidence — the only thing you can't dredge is performance on data that didn't exist when the strategy was built.

03

The ground shifts underneath the model. Markets are non-stationary — volatility regimes, correlations, even the sign of relationships change with policy and crises. In-sample fit says almost nothing about tomorrow — models tuned to the past break at the next structural shift (regime-change research). And the classic frame still holds: prices already reflect what's known (Fama).

04

Costs eat real edges, and tails do the killing. Most documented market anomalies shrink or vanish once you apply realistic trading costs, and crowding forces everyone onto noisier, earlier versions of the same signal (Novy-Marx & Velikov). Meanwhile the events that determine survival — 1998, 2008, 2020 — are far fatter and more frequent than any normal-distribution model assumes; ruin arrives in one extreme move, not a slow drip (on fat tails).

What serious people do instead of predicting

  • Manage survival before return. Avoiding ruin comes before any entry — small, bounded, pre-sized exposure that a fat-tail event can't wipe out.
  • Size small; never bet the full edge. Betting twice the "optimal" Kelly fraction erases your entire expected compounded gain; betting half of it costs only a quarter — and you're always estimating the edge, never certain of it (Thorp).
  • Diversify uncorrelated bets rather than hunt one big one — a handful of genuinely uncorrelated return streams cuts risk far more than a better single signal (Dalio's "Holy Grail").
  • Prove forward, not backward — an edge that only survives because it was tuned to history is not an edge.
  • Treat continuous research as the moat — because edges decay, the work of renewing them never ends.

So what do we actually do

We're pre-proof, and we say so. But the method isn't vague — it's the disciplined opposite of the fantasy:

We put every candidate edge through a layered false-positive filter — pre-registration, held-out testing, multiple-testing correction, deflated-Sharpe and overfitting checks, and a hard out-of-time forward gate nothing else can substitute for. We sit out when there's no edge (most days), and trade only the one regime-specific edge we've proven. We publish the killed ideas alongside the wins. And our public confidence number rises only when an edge forward-proves — never because we loosened a bar to look busy. That last rule isn't ours to bend: the researchers above are clear: fewer, slower, forward-proven edges are the correct outcome, not underperformance.
